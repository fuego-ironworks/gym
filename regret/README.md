# Regret

Collect first-person accounts of people who say that college, graduate school, doctoral training, or an academic career did not become what they expected.

The point is not to prove that education is regrettable. The point is to preserve the downside stories that institutional marketing, alumni profiles, and retrospective success narratives tend not to foreground.

## Keep different claims separate

Code each source rather than collapsing everything into "regret":

- `explicit_degree_regret` — says they regret earning/enrolling in the degree;
- `career_regret` — regrets the career path that followed;
- `financial_regret` — says the cost/debt was not worth the result;
- `expectation_mismatch` — says the experience/outcome differed materially from what they expected or were led to expect;
- `left_academia` — abandoned an academic career;
- `would_choose_differently` — says they would make another educational/career choice now;
- `payer_regret` — parent/family/payer says the education was not worth the price;
- `no_regret_but_warning` — explicitly does not regret the education while warning that common career claims are misleading;
- `mixed` — materially conflicting views;
- `unknown`.

Do not rewrite dissatisfaction as regret if the author did not say that.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer:

1. first-person essays, interviews, memoirs, posts, letters, or comments;
2. contemporaneous accounts;
3. archived versions of deleted material;
4. reporting that directly quotes the person.

For anonymous or pseudonymous comments, preserve the text, date, URL/archive URL, and context when available. Do not upgrade an unverifiable story into a biographical fact.

## Initial graduate-school corpus

### William Pannapacker / Thomas H. Benton

Pannapacker is useful because the record stretches over decades.

- 2003: "So You Want to Go to Grad School?" warned students about humanities graduate education.
- 2009: "Graduate School in the Humanities: Just Don't Go" explicitly argued that prospective humanities Ph.D. students were not being given an adequate picture of the academic labor system.
- 2021: after attaining tenure and an endowed professorship, he wrote about leaving academe.
- 2022: he described himself as still recovering from academe after leaving a career with more than 200 publications and $2.3 million in grants.
- 2023: he wrote that many Ph.D.s never get faculty careers and described the difficulty of translating a long academic career into ordinary nonacademic hiring.

Sources:
- https://digitalcommons.hope.edu/faculty_publications/743/
- https://www.chronicle.com/article/graduate-school-in-the-humanities-just-dont-go/
- https://www.chronicle.com/article/on-why-im-leaving-academe
- https://www.chronicle.com/article/still-recovering-from-academe-one-year-later
- https://www.chronicle.com/article/leaving-academe-you-need-more-than-transferable-skills/

This should be coded as a long-running critique and career-exit case. Do not claim that Pannapacker regrets receiving his own Ph.D. unless a source says that directly.

### Jennifer Polk

In a 2024 conversation, Polk said that after finishing a history Ph.D. at 32 she discovered she had no meaningful prospects for a faculty career and described telling people she was a "loser with a Ph.D.," half joking.

Source:
- https://www.chronicle.com/article/how-to-pilot-a-postacademic-career

Code as `expectation_mismatch` / `left_academia` unless a stronger direct regret statement is found.

### L. Maren Wood

Wood is an important counterexample. She wrote that most Ph.D.s she had interviewed did **not** regret earning the degree, while arguing that claims that a doctorate opens nonacademic career doors are misleading. She distinguished careers attained **after** a Ph.D. from careers attained **because of** a Ph.D.

Source:
- https://www.chronicle.com/article/odds-are-your-doctorate-will-not-prepare-you-for-a-profession-outside-academe/

Code as `no_regret_but_warning`.

## Other kinds of regret

This branch should eventually include:

- bachelor's-degree regret;
- master's-degree regret;
- professional-school regret;
- parent/payer regret over expensive colleges;
- debt stories;
- people who completed degrees but never entered the promised field;
- people who entered the field and quickly left;
- people who explicitly say the education was worthwhile despite poor financial return.

A remembered anecdote is a lead, not evidence. For example, the old online comment about a father who paid for Yale for his daughters and later said it was not worth it should remain an **unverified lead** until the original or an archive is found.
