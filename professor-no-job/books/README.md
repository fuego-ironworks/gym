# Books

Books are a second evidence stream for the `professor-no-job` branch.

CVs answer: **what jobs did this person hold?**

Books can answer a different question: **how do academics and other credentialed observers describe work, class, status, and people whose lives are mostly absent from the published record?**

## Representation rule

Keep three voices separate:

1. **author's own experience**
2. **quoted or paraphrased subjects**
3. **our interpretation**

Do not turn an author's paraphrase into a subject's direct statement.

A recurring problem worth recording is **voice asymmetry**. A professor, journalist, or graduate student can spend time with people who do ordinary paid work, write the durable book about them, and become the voice that libraries, syllabi, search indexes, and later language models preserve. The people described may leave little comparable searchable text of their own.

That does not make ethnography worthless. It makes authorship and transmission part of the evidence.

## Questions for each book

- What work has the author personally done?
- What work do the people being described do?
- Who is speaking in the crucial passage?
- Who gets to interpret whom?
- What class/status differences does the author notice?
- Does the author cross an occupational boundary or merely observe it?
- What was the material support for writing the book?
- Which voices become durable because they entered print?
- What evidence survives from the subjects independently of the author?

## Historical cross-reference

The old class question belongs here as a useful conceptual tag:

> When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?

The line is traditionally associated with John Ball and the English rising of 1381. Use it as a prompt about the distinction between those who perform necessary labor and those whose social status historically marked them as exempt from it—not as a claim that every modern academic literally fits the medieval category.
