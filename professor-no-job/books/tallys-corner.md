# Elliot Liebow — *Tally's Corner*

## Why pair it with *Cop in the Hood*

*Tally's Corner* grew from Elliot Liebow's participant-observation research among Black men around a Washington, D.C. street corner in the early 1960s.

The men included construction workers, truck drivers, janitors, dishwashers, clerks, intermittently employed men, and unemployed men. The book contains a chapter explicitly titled **“Men and Jobs.”**

## Voice asymmetry

The book is valuable partly because Liebow spent long periods with the men and recorded details that aggregate statistics miss.

But it also illustrates a structural asymmetry:

- Tally and the other men supplied much of the lived experience;
- Liebow selected, organized, interpreted, and published it;
- the published author becomes searchable, citable, teachable, and eventually machine-readable;
- most of the subjects do not leave an equivalent corpus in which they interpret Liebow or academia.

So preserve two questions at once:

1. What does the ethnography teach us about the men's work and social world?
2. Why is the ethnographer's account the durable “official” text through which later readers encounter them?

Do not treat this as proof that Liebow misrepresented his subjects. It is a question about who possesses the institutional means to produce durable knowledge.

## Work/status connection

One of Liebow's central observations is that the men's relationship to work cannot be separated from wages, insecurity, family roles, and social valuation. A Google Books excerpt summarizes his argument that the men can literally see society's valuation of their jobs in the amount employers pay.

Source:
https://books.google.com/books/about/Tally_s_Corner.html?id=Mc5HBbE8rucC

That makes the book useful beside `professor-no-job`: compare occupations whose status and compensation are discussed from above with the occupational history of the person doing the discussing.
