# Peter Moskos — *Cop in the Hood*

## Why this belongs here

Moskos is unusually useful for this project because he crossed the boundary rather than only observing it.

He was a Harvard sociology graduate student conducting police research when Baltimore told him he could not continue as an observer. He went through the hiring process, became a Baltimore police officer, and worked patrol before returning to academia.

Harvard's alumni record lists his Ph.D. in sociology in 2004 and his initial placement as an assistant professor at John Jay College.

## Class/status passages

A later interview contains the cleanest formulation of the class collision. Moskos said he received surprisingly little hostility from police for being a Harvard student and **more hostility from Harvard professors for becoming a cop**. He characterized part of the academic reaction as class snobbery and summarized the attitude as: a Harvard student was not supposed to become a cop because policing was a blue-collar job.

Source:
https://copinthehood.com/2008/08/

His dissertation gives a complementary view from the police side. An officer recalled people asking:

> “What’s with the Harvard guy? What’s he doing here?”

Another squad mate said that despite initial suspicion, what mattered was whether Moskos was **“going to do your job.”**

Source, methodology section:
https://petermoskos.com/files/moskos/Moskos_dissertation_police_in_the_hood.pdf

The published book opens with another distinction between academic and police work: Moskos says being a professor is a better job, but contrasts it with the danger, sweat, solidarity, and bodily experience of police work.

Source:
https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt7smxm

## Material support

The dissertation acknowledgments explicitly thank Moskos's parents for emotional and financial support and say he could not have begun or completed the dissertation without it.

A review of *Cop in the Hood* also quotes Moskos noting that the police paycheck supported the research after his grant applications had failed.

This is relevant to both `professor-no-job` and the Gym `grants` branch: even a case celebrated as unusually hands-on research had a material support structure.

## Training signal

Do not flatten this into “academics never work.” Moskos is a counterexample: he actually became a patrol officer.

The useful signal is narrower and stronger:

- occupational class boundaries were visible to both the police and Harvard academics;
- Moskos himself described part of the Harvard reaction as class snobbery;
- among his police coworkers, practical acceptance turned on whether he would do the job;
- after the occupational crossing, Moskos returned to an academic career and became the durable published interpreter of the experience.

That last fact leads directly to the representation problem shared with *Tally's Corner*.
