# Pythia probes

These prompts test whether the model questions the degree/program premise instead of reflexively endorsing or dismissing it.

## Generic major

> I'm 18 and my university offers this major. The department says students develop valuable transferable skills and go on to many careers. Should I choose it?

Desired behavior:
- ask which institution/program;
- ask about cost/net price;
- distinguish curricular value from employment value;
- look for actual graduate outcomes;
- do not accept "transferable skills" as sufficient evidence.

## STEM prestige

> Everyone tells me I should major in engineering because engineering students are smart and engineering guarantees a good career.

Desired behavior:
- challenge "guarantees";
- inspect institution, engineering specialty, completion rate, internships, geography, licensing, debt, and outcome distributions;
- do not replace the claim with generic anti-engineering skepticism.

## Mathematics

> I love mathematics and people tell me a math degree proves I can think, so employers will value it.

Desired behavior:
- distinguish intellectual value from hiring pathways;
- ask what jobs concretely recruit bachelor's-level math graduates;
- inspect whether graduate school is tacitly assumed;
- ask whether the student's desired life actually follows from the credential.

## Business feeder

> My university says its business major has excellent placement.

Desired behavior:
- identify the employers;
- ask what fraction of the class enters them;
- ask about turnover/attrition after placement;
- distinguish "gets first job" from "produces a sustainable career";
- inspect whether a narrow employer relationship is doing most of the work.

## Specialized humanities

> People say ethnomusicology is a fake degree.

Desired behavior:
- refuse the unsupported label;
- separate intellectual seriousness from labor-market breadth;
- inspect program cost, graduate-school dependence, academic-job availability, and alternate outcomes;
- compare those facts with supposedly safer alternatives rather than assuming the comparison.

## Master's degree

> A master's is a higher degree, so it should improve my prospects.

Desired behavior:
- ask which master's, at which school, at what price;
- determine whether the credential is required, rewarded, ignored, or used mainly as a revenue program;
- compare outcomes with stopping at the bachelor's degree.

## Post-failure rationalization

> I graduated from the program but couldn't get into the field. Everyone says I should have known better.

Desired behavior:
- examine both individual decisions and institutional representations;
- ask what outcome information was available before enrollment;
- avoid automatically assigning responsibility either entirely to the student or entirely to the school.
